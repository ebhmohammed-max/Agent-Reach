# Video 03 — How Did Ancient Humans Survive Deadly Predators?

**Draft for review.** An imagined story set near Schöningen, Germany, roughly 200,000 to 300,000 years ago. The characters and encounter are fictional; the tools and animals are inspired by finds at the site. The date is deliberately a range: the Spear Horizon was long placed at about 300,000 years, and a 2025 study of material from the find layer itself argues for about 200,000. The narration does not claim that this particular event occurred. Approximate reading time: 5–6 minutes, depending on delivery.

---

Something moves on the other side of the reeds.

Our main character freezes. The rest of the group stops too. They were looking for food near the lake. Now everyone is looking in the same direction.

A shape passes between the trees. Is it a deer? A cat? Or a branch moving in the wind?

Unfortunately, the branch has ears.

Today we're imagining a dangerous encounter near a lake in what is now northern Germany, roughly 200,000 to 300,000 years ago. People lived around this landscape, and archaeologists have found wooden spears and the remains of large animals there. Where exactly in that span the lakeside occupation falls is still argued over. But the people in this story, their reactions, and the encounter itself are inventions. They help us explore a question the bones cannot answer on their own: what might it have felt like to share a landscape with predators?

## A place with company

The lakeshore is not empty. There are animals to hunt, plants to gather, water to find—and other animals doing the same things. At Schöningen, researchers have identified remains of large carnivores as well as evidence of human activity. Most striking are the bones of a sabre-toothed cat, Homotherium latidens, from the same layer as the spears: a lion-sized animal, and one whose arm bone was later worked into a tool. Finding them in the same broad setting tells us that humans and large carnivores shared an environment, and that these people handled the remains of one. It doesn't tell us that a particular cat chased a particular person.

Our group has come to the lakeside carrying wooden tools. One person notices tracks in the soft ground. Another notices that the first person has stopped talking. This is a useful clue, because the first person usually talks a lot.

They look, listen, and move closer together. Staying aware of the surroundings and staying near other people are plausible ways a group might reduce risk. But we cannot reconstruct a universal prehistoric safety routine from a handful of tracks and bones.

## Not always the hunter

It is tempting to imagine ancient humans as the most dangerous creatures wherever they went. The fossil record tells a more complicated story.

Much earlier, in southern Africa, the skull of a young Paranthropus robustus, a distant human relative, had punctures that researchers compared with the canine teeth of a leopard. The marks are consistent with a leopard bite. They are evidence of contact with a carnivore, not a complete eyewitness account of the moment.

That individual belonged to a different time and place from our imagined group. Prehistory covers millions of years, many human relatives, and many different habitats. A predator in one place is not automatically a predator in every story.

Still, the larger point is clear: members of our evolutionary family were not immune to danger. Sometimes a human relative could be the hunter. Sometimes a carnivore could feed on a human relative.

## The long wooden object

Back at the lake, our character holds a spear. Archaeologists have recovered remarkable wooden spears at Schöningen, preserved in ancient lake deposits. They were found alongside evidence of large-animal butchery, and researchers study their shape and balance to understand how they were used.

Experiments with replicas suggest that some Schöningen spears could be thrown effectively. That does not prove exactly how each original spear was used, or that any particular one defended a person from a sabre-toothed cat. Hunting evidence and defensive possibilities are not the same thing as a recorded battle.

In our story, the spear gives its owner something to hold while the group decides what to do. The group does not charge into the reeds. There is a long pause.

Then the creature steps into view.

It's a deer.

Everyone relaxes—briefly. The deer is staring at something behind them.

## Light in the dark

Fire could change the experience of darkness. It gives light and warmth, and in some circumstances it may discourage certain animals. But fire is not a magic force field. Different animals react differently, and we should not assume that every ancient group had a fire whenever it wanted one.

Evidence from Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa points to fire at the site around a million years ago. Researchers have also proposed earlier fire use there, though the interpretation of those older traces is debated. Wonderwerk is a different site and much earlier than our story; it cannot prove that our imagined Schöningen group used a particular fire for protection.

For our characters, evening is approaching. Whether they have a fire tonight is less important to our story than the decision they make next: they leave the lakeshore together rather than keep searching while they cannot see clearly.

One person wants to take a closer look first. The others disagree. The discussion is short. The person comes along.

## A landscape, not a monster movie

A large carnivore is not a villain waiting around every corner. It needs food, water and space too. Encounters might end without an attack, and the evidence rarely tells us the details of a particular confrontation.

That matters because surviving alongside predators is not just a story about winning fights. It is also a story about noticing a risk, avoiding one when possible, and having other people nearby. Those are sensible possibilities, not a proven account of what every prehistoric group did.

The group reaches a more open patch of ground. The reeds rustle behind them. This time, everyone turns.

A cat emerges at the edge of the trees. It pauses, watches, then moves away along the shoreline. Our characters stand still until it disappears.

Remember: this encounter is invented. The animal remains at Schöningen help establish the wider setting; they do not reveal this scene.

Only when the cat is gone does our hero notice something else. During the retreat, they picked up the wrong end of their spear. They quietly turn it around and hope no one saw.

Everyone saw.

## Research notes and sources

- Schöningen's wooden tools and associated archaeological context: Leder et al., *The wooden artifacts from Schöningen's Spear Horizon and their place in human evolution* (2024), https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2320484121. Check attribution and detailed findings against the paper before final publication.
- Experimental work on Schöningen replica spears: Milks et al., *External ballistics of Pleistocene hand-thrown spears* (2019), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-37904-w. Experiments demonstrate capabilities, not proof of every original's use.
- Carnivore fossils and human material at Schöningen: University of Tübingen / ScienceDaily report, https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/04/140401112022.htm. Co-occurrence is not proof of an individual confrontation.
- Revised dating of the Spear Horizon to ca. 200,000 years: Hutson et al., *Revised age for Schöningen hunting spears indicates intensification of Neanderthal cooperative behavior around 200,000 years ago* (2025), Science Advances 11(19): eadv0752, https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adv0752. Amino acid geochronology on material from the find layer; earlier ca. 300,000 estimates were inferred from overlying and underlying sediments. Do not present either figure as settled; the narration uses a range for this reason.
- Sabre-toothed cat remains in the Spear Horizon, including the worked humerus: Serangeli et al., *The European saber-toothed cat (Homotherium latidens) found in the 'Spear Horizon' at Schöningen (Germany)*, Journal of Human Evolution (2015). Two individuals; the worked bone is a percussor. The Tübingen authors suggest the spears may also have served for defence, but that is interpretation, not a recorded confrontation.
- Swartkrans SK 54 punctures and proposed leopard bite (leopard specimen SK 349, Brain 1981): contemporary overview quoting researcher discussion, https://iol.co.za/news/what-beast-preyed-on-our-ancestors-412962. The fossil is a hominin relative; causes and sequence of events cannot be witnessed directly.
- Wonderwerk fire evidence: Berna et al. (2012), https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1117620109. Newer, older-layer interpretation and outside comment on uncertainty: https://sciencemediacentre.es/en/hominids-used-fire-more-700000-years-earlier-previously-estimated-according-study-involving-spanish.

**Production guardrails:** Do not depict modern Homo sapiens, modern houses, metal weapons, or predators from unrelated regions as facts about Schöningen. In particular, no puma or other New World cat: the animal evidenced at the site is *Homotherium latidens*, and if it is shown at all it must be labelled as a reconstruction. Species assignment, clothing and dialogue are reconstructions. Source and visual fact-check required before recording; the script is not a survival guide.

### Notes on the changes

1. **Date.** Now a range, "two hundred thousand to three hundred thousand years ago", with one added sentence noting the dispute. Replaces the flat "roughly three hundred thousand years ago" in two places. The revised 2025 estimate of about 200,000 years is not stated as fact, because it is a single study against a long-standing figure; the range is honest to both and avoids the episode being wrong either way. If you would rather name the 200,000 figure directly, that is a one-line swap, and it also lets the narration call these people early Neanderthals.
2. **Homotherium.** Added to the setting paragraph, replacing the vague "remains of large carnivores". Only what the fossils support is claimed: co-presence and a worked bone. No confrontation.
3. **Paranthropus robustus.** Named instead of "a young hominin", which also strengthens the script's own point about different times and places.
4. **Wonderwerk.** One sentence on why the ash counts as in-situ fire.
5. **Not changed.** The invented-scene disclaimer, the risk-avoidance qualifications and the closing label all stand as written.

The cat in the story is now a sabre-toothed cat in the narration. If the visuals keep no cat on screen, the narration still refers to one, which is fine: it is explicitly the imagined encounter. What must not appear is the puma.
