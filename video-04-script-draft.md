# Video 04 — What Did Ancient Humans Do After Dark?

**Direction:** same as Video 03. Story first, direct address to the viewer, named characters, no disclaimers, no hedging, no lecture. Entertainment.

**Continuity:** this picks up minutes after Video 03 ends — the same family, walking home in the dark. Renn, Sena and Tep.

**Spine:** fire didn't end the danger of the night. Someone still had to stay awake. The real invention was the night shift — the first time a person lost sleep so that someone else could have some.

**Running time:** roughly 5–6 minutes.

---

## Narration script

### The hook

It's three in the morning and you're awake.

Not because anything's wrong. Because your brain has decided, for no reason it's willing to explain, that now is the time to think about a conversation you had four years ago.

You look at your phone. The blue light. The numbers. You put it down. You pick it up again.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about three in the morning. You didn't invent it. That hour has been a problem for two hundred thousand years, and for almost all of that time, there was no phone to blame.

The dark was the whole problem. And somebody had to solve it.

### The walk home

The sun has been down for an hour, and Renn's family is still half a day from home.

Renn is in front — of course he is — carrying the spear he will absolutely tell you he used heroically today. Behind him, Sena is walking with her hand on Tep's shoulder, because Tep is eight, and eight-year-olds get tired in a way that makes them walk into things.

There's no moon. There's no path, not really. There's the sound of the lake behind them and the sound of their own feet, and that is genuinely all there is.

Now, you know what happens in the dark. You've seen the films. Something jumps out.

But that's not the thing that actually got people. The dangerous part of the night wasn't the monster. It was this: they could not see. And anything that could see better than them — anything with better ears, better eyes, better instincts — had the advantage.

So they did the only sensible thing. They went to the one place where the dark stopped working.

### Fire

There's a small fire in a hollow by the water, and it's not dramatic. It's not a wall of flame. It's a pile of dry wood and a few hot stones, kept alive by people who have done this every night of their lives.

But look at what it does.

It pushes the dark back about ten paces. Inside those ten paces, things have colour again. Faces. Hands. The fish. The shape of your kid's shoulders. Outside those ten paces, the dark is still exactly where it was, waiting, and everyone in this family knows it.

Fire didn't beat the night. Fire bought them a small room in the middle of it.

And a small room is worth a lot. It's the difference between sitting in the dark listening for something, and sitting in the light telling stories about it.

### The night shift

Here's the part of this that actually matters.

A fire doesn't stay lit by itself.

Somebody has to feed it. Somebody has to sit there, awake, while everyone else sleeps, and listen. And then somebody has to relieve them, because a person cannot do that all night and still be a person in the morning.

So they took turns.

Think about how strange that is. This is the first job in human history that has nothing to do with food. It produces nothing. You can't eat it, carry it, or trade it. All it produces is the fact that other people get to close their eyes.

Renn volunteers for the first watch, because of course he does. He sits up straight. He puts the spear across his knees. He looks out at the dark with the expression of a man who is extremely good at this.

He lasts about forty minutes.

### The story

Sena takes over without saying anything, which is somehow worse than if she'd said something.

Tep won't sleep. Tep has questions. Tep always has questions.

"Why is the dark scary?"

And Sena — who is tired, and who has spent all day gutting fish, and who is not in the mood for philosophy — gives the answer that people have been giving ever since.

"Because we can't see what's in it."

Tep thinks about this. Then Tep says the thing that changes the night.

"So tell me what's in it."

And that's it. That's the beginning. Right there, by the fire, two hundred thousand years ago, a kid asks for a story so the dark stops being empty — and the adult tells one.

Not a true one. Not a lesson. A story. Something with a shape to it, that ends, that you can hold in your head instead of the dark.

She tells him about a man who followed a deer for three days. She makes the deer bigger than it was. She makes the man stupider. Tep laughs, and then Tep is asleep, and Sena is still talking for a minute after that, because it's easier to keep going than to stop.

### What the night was for

So what did they actually do after dark?

They mended things. They made things. They ate. They talked, properly, for the first time — not shouting across a hunt, but sitting close and saying the things you only say when your hands are busy and the day is over.

They told each other what happened. And because nobody was writing anything down, the telling was the only way anything got kept. The night is where memory started being a group activity.

And they looked after each other. Somebody fed the fire so somebody else could sleep. That's it. That's the whole thing. It's not a temple, it's not a ritual, it's a person sitting up so another person can close their eyes.

### Back to you

It's still three in the morning.

The phone is still there. The conversation from four years ago is still there. But somewhere under all of that, there's something much older going on — a system that decided, a very long time ago, that being awake when everyone else is asleep is a job worth doing.

Someone did that for you. For thousands of years, someone sat up so that you could sleep.

Tonight, it's your turn. You're the one awake.

So check the fire. Listen once. And then — like Sena, like every tired person who ever kept a small light going in the dark — go to sleep. Someone's covering the next watch.

## Production notes

- **Style is locked. Read `video-style-guide.md` before generating anything.** Every face must act — big oval eyes, thick eyebrows, varied mouths. Warm saturated palette, thick ink outlines. Blank disc heads with dot eyes have been rejected twice.
- Same characters and artwork style as Video 03: Renn (tall, brown horse-hide, spear, talks too much), Sena (ochre hide, practical), Tep (small child).
- Fire is warm and modest, never a wall of flame. Stones, dry wood, a hollow.
- No on-screen text cards, no labels, no disclaimers.
- The deer-and-idiot story Sena tells is the emotional centre — the artwork should show it as a story being told, not as something happening.
- Ending returns to the modern night, mirroring the opening.
